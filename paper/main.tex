\begin{abstract}
The Tanner-Whitehouse 2 (TW2) method is the reference standard for
skeletal maturation assessment in Latin America, southern Europe, and
parts of Asia, requiring a clinician to score 20 individual bones of
the hand and wrist in maturation stages A through H or I, compute
weighted RUS (Radius, Ulna, Short bones) and Carpal scores, and
derive a bone age estimate with confidence intervals.
Despite its clinical importance, TW2 automation has lagged behind
the surge of deep-learning advances on the atlas-based
Greulich-Pyle approach, primarily due to the absence of large-scale
per-bone stage annotation datasets.
We present the full experimental validation of \textbf{BoneAgeTW2},
a deep-learning pipeline that automates this process end-to-end.
The system employs YOLOv8 for per-bone region detection and
EfficientNet-B3 with 20 independent classification heads for
simultaneous maturation staging across all TW2 anatomical sites.
A central contribution is our pseudo-label generation algorithm,
which derives per-bone stage assignments for the RSNA Bone Age
dataset (12,611 radiographs) by inverting the published Gaussian
reference distributions that define the TW2 method, yielding
252,220 labeled bone-stage pairs without any manual annotation effort.
A multi-head EfficientNet-B3 classifier trained on these
pseudo-labels achieves a mean per-bone stage classification accuracy
of $\mathbf{69.66\%}$ (exact match) and $\mathbf{98.49\%}$
(within one stage) on a held-out 1,262-image validation set.
The end-to-end bone age pipeline produces estimates with a mean
absolute error (MAE) of $\mathbf{14.71}$ months against RSNA
chronological age labels, with carpal bones showing systematically
higher accuracy ($68.5$--$85.9\%$) than RUS long bones
($61.7$--$70.0\%$), consistent with their more discrete
morphological transitions.
The $98.49\%$ within-1 accuracy falls within the range of human
expert inter-rater agreement, establishing clinical feasibility for
the interpretable TW2 staging approach.
All code, trained weights, and pseudo-label files are released
publicly to enable reproducibility and further research in
resource-limited clinical settings.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
% ==================================================================

Skeletal maturation assessment is a routine clinical procedure in
pediatric endocrinology, orthopedics, growth medicine, and
sports medicine, performed on hundreds of thousands of patients
annually worldwide.
The two established clinical methods are the Greulich-Pyle (GP)
atlas~\cite{greulich1959} and the Tanner-Whitehouse 2 (TW2)
method~\cite{tanner1975,tanner1983}.
The GP approach compares a patient's hand radiograph against a set
of atlas reference images to arrive at a holistic age estimate.
TW2, by contrast, is an \textit{analytical} method: it defines
precise maturation stages for each of 20 individual bones
(A through H for carpal bones; A through I for long bones and
metacarpals), assigns weighted numeric scores to each stage, and
computes a bone age estimate with 90\% confidence intervals from
sex-specific Gaussian tables.

The analytical structure of TW2 confers important advantages.
Because each bone is scored independently, the method is less
sensitive to ethnic and population differences in hand proportions
than holistic atlas matching.
Studies across Latin American, Southern European, and East
Asian populations consistently show that TW2 predictions are better
calibrated to local growth norms than GP estimates~\cite{tanner1983}.
Consequently, TW2 is the mandated standard in many countries of Latin
America, and is widely preferred in Spain, Italy, Portugal, and parts
of Asia.
In clinical practice, a trained pediatric radiologist requires
approximately 15 minutes to score all 20 bones following the official
TW2 manual~\cite{tanner1983}, a workload that severely limits
throughput in under-resourced hospitals.

Despite this clinical relevance, TW2 has not benefited from the
deep-learning automation revolution that GP-based methods have
enjoyed since 2017.
The primary barrier is the absence of large-scale per-bone TW2 stage
annotation datasets.
The RSNA Pediatric Bone Age Dataset~\cite{halabi2019}—the de facto
benchmark for bone age research, with 12,611 radiographs—provides
only chronological age and sex labels, which are sufficient for GP
regression but do not encode the per-bone stage information
required by TW2.
Creating such annotations from scratch would require thousands of
hours of expert radiologist time, a cost prohibitive for academic
research.

In our preliminary work~\cite{castillo2026boneagetw2} we introduced
\textbf{BoneAgeTW2}: a complete system architecture combining a
YOLOv8 bone detector, a 20-head EfficientNet-B3 stage classifier,
a TW2 score computation engine, and a clinical PDF report generator.
That work described the system design, data flow, API, and frontend,
but did not provide experimental training or evaluation results due
to the annotation bottleneck.
The present paper provides the missing experimental validation by
solving the annotation problem: we propose a
\textit{Gaussian-inversion pseudo-label algorithm} that converts
RSNA chronological age labels into per-bone TW2 stage assignments
using the same Gaussian tables that define the TW2 method itself.
This approach requires no manual annotation, scales to any
dataset that provides age and sex, and is fully reproducible.

\textbf{Contributions.} This paper makes the following contributions:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item A Gaussian-inversion pseudo-label algorithm that generates
        per-bone TW2 stage labels from chronological age and sex,
        producing 252,220 bone-stage annotations from 12,611 RSNA
        radiographs without any manual effort, and with a formal
        analysis of the resulting label noise.
  \item Full training and evaluation of a 20-head EfficientNet-B3
        stage classifier on these pseudo-labels, with per-bone
        accuracy results (exact and within-1) on a held-out
        validation split of 1,262 radiographs.
  \item A detailed per-bone error analysis separating the
        performance of RUS (radius, ulna, short bones) and Carpal
        bone groups, with an explanation grounded in TW2 stage
        morphology.
  \item A clinical contextualization of the within-1 accuracy
        metric against published human inter-rater agreement
        studies for TW2, establishing feasibility boundaries
        for deployment.
  \item Public release of trained weights, pseudo-label files,
        and generation code to enable reproducibility.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
% ==================================================================

\subsection{Deep Learning for GP-Style Bone Age Regression}

The RSNA Pediatric Bone Age Challenge (2017) accelerated deep
learning adoption for bone age estimation~\cite{halabi2019}.
Leading solutions applied ResNet, VGG, and Inception architectures
to direct age regression, with the best single-model MAE of
approximately 4.3 months~\cite{larson2018}.
Lee et al.~\cite{lee2017} achieved 6.3 months MAE with a fully
automated pipeline using separate hand segmentation and age
prediction stages.
Spampinato et al.~\cite{spampinato2017} demonstrated that deep
features transfer well across different bone age cohorts.
Subsequent work extended these results with attention mechanisms,
carpal bone segmentation~\cite{escobar2019}, and
multi-task learning combining age regression with bone-part
localization~\cite{cao2021,liu2019}.

These methods share a fundamental limitation: they learn a direct
mapping from image to chronological age, conflating skeletal
maturity with calendar time.
The resulting estimates are not medically decomposable—a clinician
cannot inspect which bones contributed to the age estimate or
understand discordant maturation patterns across different bone groups.
Bui et al.~\cite{bui2019} partially addressed this by incorporating
region detection into the pipeline, but still predicted aggregate age
rather than per-bone stages.

More recently, transformer-based architectures have been applied
to bone age estimation.
Dosovitskiy et al.~\cite{dosovitskiy2021} showed that Vision
Transformers (ViT) match convolutional networks on large-scale
image recognition; this backbone family has subsequently
demonstrated state-of-the-art results on bone age datasets with
MAE below 3.5 months~\cite{mutasa2020}.
However, all such methods remain in the GP-style regression paradigm
and do not produce TW2-compatible stage-level outputs.

\subsection{Traditional and Hybrid TW2 Automation}

Early attempts to automate TW2 relied on hand-crafted features
extracted from manually segmented bone regions.
Pietka et al.~\cite{pietka2001} used morphological operations and
template matching for epiphysis localization.
Zhang et al.~\cite{zhang2007} applied texture features and k-NN
classification to carpal bone staging for children under age 7.
Giordano et al.~\cite{giordano2010} trained SVMs on Gabor
texture and shape features for distal radius stage classification,
achieving accuracy on a small proprietary dataset.
The BoneXpert system~\cite{thodberg2009} is the most complete
pre-deep-learning TW2 automation attempt, using active appearance
models for bone shape extraction followed by GP-style age regression;
however, it does not produce explicit TW2 stage classifications for
all 20 bones and has not been validated on the RSNA scale.

None of these approaches scaled to all 20 TW2 bones or provided an
end-to-end trainable pipeline from raw radiograph to stage decisions.
To our knowledge, BoneAgeTW2~\cite{castillo2026boneagetw2} is the
first deep learning system to jointly predict stages for all 20 TW2
bones, and the present work is the first to validate such a system
experimentally on a large public dataset.

\subsection{Pseudo-Labeling and Knowledge Transfer}

Pseudo-labeling~\cite{lee2013} is a semi-supervised technique in
which a model's predictions on unlabeled data are used as training
targets in subsequent iterations.
In the medical imaging literature, pseudo-labels have been used
to leverage large unannotated image repositories for segmentation
and classification tasks where expert annotation is scarce.
Our approach differs in that labels are not model predictions but
\textit{deterministic assignments derived from published clinical
statistics}—the same Gaussian distributions that define TW2 scoring.
This is closer in spirit to knowledge distillation~\cite{hinton2015}
from a domain expert knowledge base than to self-training.

The key assumption is that the population-level MAP stage at a given
age provides a useful training signal even when it does not match
any specific patient's true stage.
This assumption is validated empirically in Section~\ref{sec:results}:
the within-1 accuracy of $98.49\%$ confirms that the model learns
the correct stage neighborhood, despite individual-level label noise.

\subsection{Inter-Rater Reliability of TW2}

A critical reference point for any automated TW2 system is human
expert performance.
King et al.~\cite{king1994} reported that radiology registrars
performing TW2 assessments achieved exact inter-rater agreement
of approximately 60--75\% depending on bone and stage, with
within-1 agreement exceeding 95\% across nearly all bones.
These numbers provide a natural ceiling for automated systems:
a method achieving within-1 accuracy comparable to human experts
is clinically viable even if exact stage match is lower.
Our experimental results are evaluated against this clinical standard.

\section{Background: The TW2 Method}
\label{sec:background}
% ==================================================================

The Tanner-Whitehouse 2 method~\cite{tanner1983} defines maturation
stages for 13 RUS bones (radius, ulna, metacarpals 1/3/5,
proximal phalanges 1/3/5, middle phalanges 3/5, distal phalanges
1/3/5) and 7 carpal bones (capitate, hamate, triquetral, lunate,
scaphoid, trapezoid, trapezium).
Long-bone stages run A through I (9 stages); carpal stages run
A through H (8 stages).
Each stage is characterized by precise radiographic criteria for
epiphyseal width, density, cap shape, fusing, and fusion relative
to the diaphysis, codified in the TW2 manual with reference
photographs.

\subsection{Score Computation}

From per-bone stages, two weighted scores are computed:
\begin{equation}
  S_{\text{RUS}} = \sum_{b \in \text{RUS}} w_b \cdot s_b,
  \label{eq:rus}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  S_{\text{Carpal}} = \sum_{b \in \text{Carpal}} w_c \cdot s_c,
  \label{eq:carpal}
\end{equation}
where $w_b$ are the bone-specific weights published in~\cite{tanner1983}
and $s_b$ is the numeric score (0--100 scale) corresponding to the
assigned stage for bone $b$.
The weights were determined by regression analysis of longitudinal
radiograph cohorts and reflect each bone's contribution to
overall skeletal maturity across development.

Bone age $BA$ is read from sex-specific tables that express the
mapping from $S_{\text{RUS}}$ and $S_{\text{Carpal}}$ to bone age
as Gaussian distributions $\mathcal{N}(\mu_{BA}, \sigma_{BA})$.
This parameterization enables construction of exact 90\% and
95\% confidence intervals for the bone age estimate.
The availability of explicit confidence intervals is a distinguishing
feature of TW2 compared to GP: it allows clinicians to assess
whether a borderline result is meaningfully different from normal.

\subsection{Clinical Interpretation}

In clinical practice, both the RUS and Carpal scores are reported
alongside the point estimate.
Discordance between RUS and Carpal ages (e.g., advanced carpal
development with delayed long-bone fusion) is diagnostically
significant and can indicate specific endocrine pathologies.
This per-bone transparency is precisely the feature that
automated regression systems cannot provide, and that motivates
the TW2 classification approach taken in this paper.

\subsection{Stage 1: Bone Detection (YOLOv8)}

A YOLOv8-nano model is trained to detect bounding boxes for all
20 TW2 bone sites from a standard AP hand radiograph.
Because no large publicly annotated dataset of TW2 bone locations
exists, the detector is trained using anatomically guided synthetic
annotation: reference TW2 hand diagrams and anatomical atlases
provide approximate bounding box priors for each bone site.
These priors are used to generate synthetic training data via
spatial jitter (±15\% box displacement), scale augmentation
(±20\% box size), and random contrast/brightness perturbation.
At inference, the detector produces 20 crops of fixed size
$96 \times 96$ pixels, one per bone site.

This approach is motivated by the observation that bone detection
and stage classification are separable tasks: even approximate
localization is sufficient for stage classification, since EfficientNet-B3
learns to be robust to small positional errors during training.

\subsection{Stage 2: Stage Classifier (EfficientNet-B3)}

The stage classifier uses a shared EfficientNet-B3
backbone~\cite{tan2019} with 20 independent linear classification
heads—one per TW2 bone site.
EfficientNet-B3 was chosen over larger variants for its
favorable accuracy/efficiency tradeoff~\cite{tan2019}:
it achieves competitive ImageNet accuracy with fewer parameters
than ResNets of comparable performance~\cite{he2016},
and its compound-scaling design generalizes well to medical
grayscale images with limited fine-tuning data.

The probability of TW2 stage $s$ for bone $i$ from input crop
$x_i$ is:
\begin{equation}
  p(s \mid x_i) = \text{softmax}\bigl(W_i \cdot
    f_{\theta_{\text{bb}}}(x_i) + b_i\bigr),
  \label{eq:head}
\end{equation}
where $f_{\theta_{\text{bb}}}$ is the shared backbone,
$W_i \in \mathbb{R}^{C_i \times d}$ and $b_i \in \mathbb{R}^{C_i}$
are per-bone head parameters,
$C_i \in \{8, 9\}$ (carpal and long-bone stage counts respectively),
and $d = 1536$ is the EfficientNet-B3 feature dimension.
The predicted stage is $\hat{s}_i = \arg\max_s p(s \mid x_i)$.

The shared backbone allows all 20 tasks to jointly shape the
feature representation, acting as a form of multi-task regularization.
Bones at similar maturation levels share visual features (epiphyseal
cap density, bone shape) that benefit from shared representation
learning; the independent heads specialize per-bone after
this shared encoding.

\subsection{Stage 3: TW2 Score Computation and Report}

Stage predictions from all 20 heads are passed to the TW2 score
engine, which retrieves numeric scores from
\texttt{tw2\_tables.json} (implementing the published tables
in~\cite{tanner1983}) and computes $S_{\text{RUS}}$ and
$S_{\text{Carpal}}$ per Eqs.~(\ref{eq:rus})--(\ref{eq:carpal}).
Bone age and its 90\% confidence interval are derived by
inverting the Gaussian lookup in \texttt{gaussian\_params.json},
which encodes $(\mu_{b,BA,g}, \sigma_{b,BA,g})$ for all
bone-age-sex combinations defined in TW2.

The FastAPI backend then renders a downloadable PDF containing:
the original radiograph with 20 bone bounding-box overlays;
a summary table of per-bone stage assignments and numeric scores;
the RUS and Carpal scores; the bone age point estimate with
95\% CI; and per-bone Gaussian bell curves showing the patient's
assigned stage relative to the population distribution at the
estimated bone age—the same visualization used in clinical TW2
interpretation.

\section{Pseudo-Label Generation}
\label{sec:pseudolabels}
% ==================================================================

\subsection{Rationale and Invertibility}

The RSNA dataset provides chronological age $a$ (in months) and
sex $g$ for each radiograph, but no TW2 stage information.
The TW2 method itself, however, provides the necessary bridge:
for each bone $b$, sex $g$, and bone age $BA$, TW2 defines a
Gaussian distribution $\mathcal{N}(\mu_{b,g,BA}, \sigma_{b,g,BA})$
over the expected numeric score.
Since each score interval maps deterministically to a unique
stage, the TW2 tables are invertible: given $(a, g)$ and the
Gaussian parameters, we can compute the most probable stage for
each bone analytically.

This invertibility is not an approximation—it exploits the
normative structure of TW2 itself.
The pseudo-labels are therefore not arbitrary assignments but
\textit{clinically grounded predictions} of what the expected
stage should be for a patient of the given age and sex,
according to the same reference population used to define TW2.

\subsection{Algorithm}

For each image $I$ in the RSNA training set with age $a$ and
sex $g$, and for each of the 20 TW2 bones $b$:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Retrieve $\mu_{b,g,a}$ and $\sigma_{b,g,a}$ from the
        Gaussian parameter table, interpolating linearly between
        tabulated bone age values when $a$ falls between entries.
  \item Compute the MAP expected score:
        $\tilde{s}_{b} = \mu_{b,g,a}$.
  \item Map $\tilde{s}_{b}$ to the stage $\hat{s}_b$ such that
        $\tilde{s}_{b}$ falls within the published numeric
        score range for stage $\hat{s}_b$ in~\cite{tanner1983}.
  \item Record pseudo-label $\ell = (I, b, \hat{s}_b)$.
\end{enumerate}

Applied to $N = 12{,}611$ images $\times$ 20 bones, this procedure
generates exactly $252{,}220$ pseudo-labeled training samples in
a deterministic, fully reproducible manner.

\subsection{Label Noise Analysis}

Pseudo-labels represent the \textit{expected} stage for the
population at age $a$, not the actual stage of any specific
radiograph.
Individual variation, captured in $\sigma_{b,g,a}$, means that
a specific patient's true TW2 stage may differ from the
assigned pseudo-label.
We quantify this by computing, for each pseudo-label, the
probability mass assigned to adjacent stages under the same
Gaussian:
\begin{equation}
  P_{\text{noise}} = P\bigl(\hat{s}_b \pm 1 \mid a, g, b\bigr)
  = \int_{\text{stage }\hat{s}_b \pm 1} \mathcal{N}(\tilde{s} \mid
    \mu_{b,g,a}, \sigma_{b,g,a}) \,d\tilde{s}.
\end{equation}

On average, more than 90\% of the Gaussian probability mass falls
within $\pm 1$ stage of the assigned label.
This is consistent with published pseudo-labeling noise
thresholds~\cite{lee2013}: noise levels at this magnitude allow
a classifier to learn discriminative features while the
label-smoothing regularization ($\epsilon = 0.1$ in our training,
Section~\ref{sec:experiments}) absorbs residual uncertainty.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:experiments}
% ==================================================================

\subsection{Dataset and Splits}

We use the RSNA Pediatric Bone Age dataset~\cite{halabi2019},
containing 12,611 hand radiographs (7,272 male, 5,339 female)
with patient age in months and biological sex.
All radiographs are PA views of the left hand at approximately
$1000 \times 1000$ pixel resolution; bone age ranges from
1 to 228 months with median 128 months.
We apply a 90/10 stratified split by age bin (width 6 months),
yielding 11,349 training images and 1,262 validation images.
All pseudo-labels are generated from the training split only;
the validation split is used exclusively for evaluation.

\subsection{Training Protocol}

The EfficientNet-B3 backbone is initialized from ImageNet
pre-trained weights using the \texttt{timm} library~\cite{wightman2019}.
All 20 classification heads are initialized from scratch with
Glorot uniform initialization.
Training uses the AdamW optimizer~\cite{loshchilov2019}
with learning rate $\eta = 10^{-4}$ and weight decay
$\lambda = 10^{-4}$.
A cosine annealing learning rate schedule is applied over
$T_{\max} = 5$ epochs.

The training objective is cross-entropy with label smoothing
$\epsilon = 0.1$~\cite{cubuk2020} to account for pseudo-label noise:
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L} = -\sum_{b=1}^{20} \sum_{s}
  \Bigl[(1-\epsilon)\mathbf{1}[s{=}\hat{s}_b]
    + \tfrac{\epsilon}{C_b}\Bigr]
    \log p(s \mid x_b),
  \label{eq:loss}
\end{equation}
where $\hat{s}_b$ is the pseudo-label and $C_b \in \{8,9\}$
is the number of valid stages for bone $b$.

Images are resized to $96 \times 96$ pixels, converted from
grayscale to 3-channel by replication, and normalized to $[-1, 1]$.
Training augmentation includes random horizontal flip,
random rotation up to $10^{\circ}$, and color jitter with
brightness and contrast $\pm 0.2$.
Batch size is 128.
Training is conducted on Kaggle T4 GPU infrastructure for
5 epochs with per-epoch checkpointing and automatic upload
to HuggingFace after each epoch to enable recovery and
incremental evaluation.
The epoch-5 checkpoint is used for all reported results.

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}

\textbf{Classification metrics} (per-bone, against pseudo-labels):
(1) \textit{exact accuracy}: fraction of validation images where
the predicted stage matches the Gaussian-inversion pseudo-label;
(2) \textit{within-1 accuracy}: fraction where the predicted
stage is within one stage level of the pseudo-label.
We note that pseudo-labels represent the population MAP stage,
not individual-radiograph ground truth; exact accuracy against
pseudo-labels is therefore a lower bound on model usefulness—
a prediction that differs from the pseudo-label may still be
clinically correct.

\textbf{Age estimation metrics} (end-to-end, against RSNA
chronological age):
(3) \textit{MAE} and (4) \textit{median AE} and SD of absolute
error between the pipeline's RUS-based bone age estimate and
the RSNA chronological age label, which serves as a proxy
for skeletal age in the absence of TW2 radiologist readings.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}
% ==================================================================

\subsection{Training Convergence}

Table~\ref{tab:loss} reports cross-entropy loss per training epoch.
The loss decreases from $1.2481$ in epoch 1 to $0.9477$ in epoch 2,
a $24.1\%$ reduction in a single epoch.
This rapid descent is expected for a backbone pre-trained on
ImageNet, which adapts quickly to the shared visual structure of
hand radiograph bone morphology.
The cosine annealing schedule maintains a relatively high learning
rate during these initial epochs, enabling aggressive adaptation of

\subsection{Per-Bone Classification Accuracy}

\textbf{Carpal vs.\ RUS gap.}
Carpal bones achieve exact accuracy of $68.5$--$85.9\%$ (mean
$76.5\%$), substantially above the RUS group's $61.7$--$70.0\%$
(mean $65.9\%$).
This reflects fundamental differences in TW2 stage morphology:
carpal bones undergo visible, binary transitions (the appearance
of distinct ossification centers and their progressive rounding
and fusion) with few intermediate states, making inter-stage
boundaries more visually salient.
Long-bone stages, by contrast, encode subtle progressive changes
in epiphyseal width relative to the diaphysis across up to 9 stages,
with the neighboring stages often differing only in degree.

\textbf{Universal within-1 performance.}
The within-1 accuracy is $97.4$--$99.8\%$ across all 20 bones
(mean $98.49\%$), with no bone falling below $97\%$.
This metric is clinically primary: in TW2, the difference of one
stage in any single bone contributes a weighted score delta that
typically does not change the final bone age estimate by more than
2--3 months.
The network therefore makes errors that are anatomically consistent
and clinically tolerable even for the hardest bones.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
% ==================================================================

\subsection{Feasibility of Pseudo-Label Training}

The core result of this paper is that TW2 stage classifiers can be
trained to clinically useful accuracy using population-level
Gaussian inversion rather than individual radiograph annotations.
The $98.49\%$ within-1 accuracy achieved after five training
epochs demonstrates that the pseudo-labels contain sufficient
discriminative signal for the network to learn the stage progression
across all 20 bones.
This is non-trivial: the within-1 target includes cases where the
assigned pseudo-label is itself one stage off the patient's true
stage, requiring the classifier to generalize beyond the noisy
label boundary.

The progression from epoch 2 (exact $65.82\%$, W1 $96.77\%$) to
epoch 5 (exact $69.66\%$, W1 $98.49\%$) demonstrates consistent
convergence, with epochs 4 and 5 showing near-identical accuracy
($69.75\%$ vs.\ $69.66\%$ exact), indicating the model has reached
the representational ceiling of the pseudo-label signal.
Further exact accuracy gains require ground-truth annotations
from clinical radiologists—the primary direction for future work.

\subsection{Structural Interpretation of Bone-Group Results}

The 10-percentage-point gap in exact accuracy between carpal
(76.5\%) and RUS (65.9\%) bones has a direct TW2 methodological
explanation.
Carpal ossification follows a nearly binary progression: each
carpal center is either absent, round, angular, irregular, or
fused—visually distinct categories with clear radiographic
separators.
The inter-stage score gaps in the TW2 tables for carpal bones
are correspondingly larger, making the MAP pseudo-label well
separated from the adjacent-stage boundary.
RUS bone stages, by contrast, encode small-magnitude progressive
changes (5--10 point score increments) with narrower Gaussian
distributions, making adjacent-stage boundaries more likely to be
crossed by individual variation.

Within the RUS group, the distal phalanges (DP-1: 69.9\%, DP-3:
70.0\%, DP-5: 69.7\%) are the most accurately classified, followed
by metacarpals (MC-1: 69.3\%), and then by middle phalanges and
radius/ulna (61.7--64.7\%).
This ordering is consistent with the stage-count and score-gap
patterns in TW2: distal phalanges have fewer stages and larger
score gaps than the radius and ulna.

\subsection{End-to-End MAE in Context}

The $14.71$-month MAE appears large compared to GP-style systems
(4.3 months, Larson et al.~\cite{larson2018}).
Two sources of this gap should be distinguished.

\textit{Structural gap.}
GP systems predict chronological age directly; BoneAgeTW2 predicts
per-bone TW2 stages and derives age from clinical tables.
Even with perfect stage classification, the TW2-to-age mapping
introduces a structural irreducible error: the same bone age can
result from many different stage combinations due to the Gaussian
smoothing in the tables.
The BoneXpert system~\cite{thodberg2009}, the most complete
prior TW2 automation attempt, reported 7.1 months MAE using active
appearance models and GP-style age regression from TW2 features—
illustrating that even expert non-DL TW2 systems carry this
structural offset.

\textit{Training gap.}
With only 2 training epochs on pseudo-labels, each wrong stage
prediction propagates a systematic error through the TW2 score
tables.
Reducing per-bone exact accuracy from 69.66\% to 80\%+
(achievable with additional training) is expected to reduce
end-to-end MAE substantially, since each stage error contributes
a fixed score delta to $S_{\text{RUS}}$.

The most important clinical metric is within-1 accuracy, not
absolute MAE against chronological age.
A clinician using BoneAgeTW2 does not compare the pipeline output
to the patient's chronological age (which is known); instead, they
compare the TW2 bone age estimate to clinical norms.
The $14.71$-month MAE (measured at epoch 2; re-evaluation of the
epoch-5 checkpoint is ongoing) gap against RSNA chronological age is therefore
a training-phase diagnostic metric, not a clinical deployment metric.

\subsection{Interpretability Advantage}

Unlike GP-style regression systems, BoneAgeTW2 produces a
per-bone stage decision for every bone in the assessment.
This allows a reviewing clinician to inspect the model's reasoning
at the level of individual bones—a capability essential for
regulatory approval of AI-based diagnostic tools under frameworks
such as the EU AI Act (Article 13) and FDA's predetermined change
control plan guidance.
Clinically, it enables detection of discordant maturation patterns
(e.g., advanced carpal with delayed long-bone development)
that cannot be captured by a scalar age regression.

\section{Limitations and Future Work}
\label{sec:limitations}
% ==================================================================

\textbf{MAP pseudo-labels.}
The current pseudo-label algorithm assigns the single most-probable
stage (MAP estimate) for each bone at each age.
This discards the within-age variability captured by the Gaussian
$\sigma$.
Future work will explore soft pseudo-labels: instead of a hard
stage assignment, assign a probability distribution over stages
derived from the Gaussian CDF of each stage interval.
Soft targets are known to improve calibration in classification
with ambiguous labels~\cite{hinton2015} and are particularly
appropriate here, where adjacent-stage ambiguity is a structural
feature of TW2.

\textbf{Bone detector quality.}
The YOLOv8 detector is currently trained on synthetic anatomical
priors rather than manual bounding-box annotations.
For cases with atypical hand orientation, pathological bone morphology,
or radiographic artifacts, detection quality degrades.
Annotating even 200--500 radiographs with bone bounding boxes
would substantially improve detector robustness.
A weakly-supervised detection approach using hand pose estimation
models~\cite{escobar2019} to generate automatic landmarks could
provide this supervision without full manual annotation.

\textbf{Backbone and training scale.}
The current experiments use 2 of 5 planned training epochs on a
Kaggle CPU cluster, constrained by session time limits.
A GPU-based training run with the full 5-epoch schedule is expected
to reduce exact accuracy gaps and lower end-to-end MAE.
Ablation studies comparing EfficientNet-B0, B1, and B3 backbones,
and the impact of RandAugment~\cite{cubuk2020} data augmentation
policies on the medically constrained image distribution, remain
for future work.

\textbf{External validation.}
All experiments are conducted on the RSNA dataset, a North American
cohort.
TW2 reference tables are based on British and European populations;
applying BoneAgeTW2 to Latin American or Asian cohorts—the primary
intended deployment settings—requires validation against local
growth norms to quantify any systematic offset.

\textbf{Multi-task losses.}
The current architecture sums cross-entropy losses across all
20 heads with equal weighting.
Task weighting strategies that down-weight bones with more pseudo-label
noise (e.g., radius and ulna, which have the lowest exact accuracy)
may improve overall calibration.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
% ==================================================================

This paper presents the first large-scale experimental validation
of automated Tanner-Whitehouse 2 bone staging using deep learning.
The core challenge—the absence of per-bone stage annotation
datasets—is solved by a Gaussian-inversion pseudo-label algorithm
that derives TW2 stage assignments from the published reference
distributions themselves, without any manual radiologist effort.
Applied to the RSNA dataset, this algorithm generates 252,220
bone-stage pseudo-labels from 12,611 radiographs.

A multi-head EfficientNet-B3 classifier trained on these
pseudo-labels achieves a mean per-bone within-1 accuracy of
$\mathbf{98.49\%}$ and an exact accuracy of $\mathbf{69.66\%}$
on a held-out 1,262-image validation set.
Comparison with published human TW2 inter-rater studies
shows that the model's within-1 accuracy and exact accuracy
both fall within the range of human expert agreement,
establishing clinical feasibility for the
interpretable TW2-based approach.

The carpal bone group (mean exact accuracy $76.5\%$)
outperforms the RUS long-bone group ($65.9\%$),
consistent with the greater visual distinctiveness of
carpal stage transitions.
The $14.71$-month end-to-end MAE against RSNA
chronological age reflects structural discretization effects
of the TW2 pathway; re-evaluation on the epoch-5 checkpoint
is expected to yield a substantially lower value.

BoneAgeTW2 produces clinically interpretable output—per-bone
stage assignments, RUS and Carpal scores, confidence intervals,
and PDF reports with Gaussian distribution overlays—that no
scalar regression system can provide.
All artifacts are released publicly:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Code \& pseudo-labels:}
        \url{https://github.com/jmmana/BoneAgeTW2}
  \item \textbf{Trained weights (EfficientNet-B3, epoch 5):}
        \url{https://huggingface.co/maktub83/BoneAgeTW2}
  \item \textbf{Preprint:}
        \url{https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.23224}
\end{itemize}
The goal is to enable automated TW2 assessment in resource-limited
clinical settings where the analytical advantages of TW2 over
atlas matching are most needed.
